# Formats

Format changes which rules bind. Read the relevant section before editing or drafting. When the format is unclear, ask.

Rules that hold everywhere: no invented facts, no weasel attribution, no fake-profound kicker, scope guards intact.

**Format not listed here?** Apply the general rules. Name the closest listed format if one is meaningfully close and say you treated it that way. If none is close, say so and identify the format-specific thing you were unsure about, so the writer can supply the convention. Do not force an analogy that does not hold, and do not invent conventions for a format this file does not cover.

---

## Email

- Lead with the ask or the answer. The reason goes second.
- Subject line states the thing, not the topic. "Need your sign-off on the Q3 budget by Thursday" beats "Q3 Budget."
- No headers. No bullet lists under four items. No bold.
- No em dashes.
- One request per email where possible. Two requests means the second gets missed.
- Cut "I hope this finds you well," "I wanted to reach out," "Just circling back," "Per my last email."
- Keep the writer's normal sign-off. Do not standardize it.
- **Cold outreach:** name the specific thing you read, built, or noticed. A cold email that passes the portability test is spam.
- **Bad news:** state it in the first two sentences. Softening the lead reads as evasion.

Length ceiling: if it runs past 150 words, it should probably be a document with a one-line email attached.

## Slack and chat

- One message, not four. Threading a monologue is worse than a paragraph.
- No headers, no bold, minimal formatting.
- Sentence fragments are fine here and are usually voice.
- Lead with what you need from the reader.

## LinkedIn

One-line stacking and hook/reveal structures are common enough here to justify format-specific bans.

- No one-line-paragraph stacking. That rhythm is the format's signature tell.
- No hook-then-line-break-then-reveal.
- No "Agree?" or "Thoughts?" closer.
- No numbered lesson lists unless the writer actually has numbered lessons.
- Concrete first-person specifics beat any framing device. What you did, what broke, what the number was.
- Keep it under 200 words unless there is real substance.

## X / Twitter

- No thread-bait openers ("A thread 🧵", "Most people get this wrong").
- Single posts over threads unless the argument genuinely needs steps.
- No em dashes, no colon reveals. Both eat characters and both read as generated.
- The last post in a thread is where fake-profound kickers live. Check it.

## Blog post

- Get to the point in the first paragraph. A personal opening is fine when it creates context or tension. Generic scene-setting is not.
- Headers only where the piece genuinely turns. Not every 200 words.
- One idea per section.
- Keep digressions that carry personality. They are usually the reason someone reads a blog instead of documentation.
- End on the last concrete point, a takeaway, or a next action.

## Long-form article or essay

- Front-load the thesis where it helps the reader. Do not force every section into the same point-detail-background shape.
- Vary paragraph length deliberately. Uniform paragraphs are the strongest structural tell in long form.
- Name sources inline. Weasel attribution is more damaging here because the reader expects rigor.
- Section endings are where puffery accumulates. Check each one.
- The subhead is not a place for colon reveals.

## Newsletter

- Sits between email and blog. Use email rules for the opening and blog rules for the body.
- No "In this issue" table of contents under four items.
- Keep the writer's running voice and recurring bits. Those are why people subscribe.

## Landing page and marketing copy

- Evaluative, comparative, performance, and outcome claims need a mechanism or a number behind them. Concrete feature and behaviour claims stand on their own: "works offline", "exports PDFs", "supports dark mode" are already checkable and need nothing added.
- Portability test per the canonical definition in `patterns.md`: reject generic framing that asserts nothing specific. A concrete feature claim ("works offline") is not empty just because a competitor shares the feature — it states something checkable.
- Second person. Present tense. No future-tense promises without a date.

## Release notes and changelogs

- What changed first. Name who is affected when known or material. State required action when there is one. Absent affected-user or action facts, a note stating only what changed and when is complete; do not block on fields the writer did not supply.
- Match tense to release state: past for shipped changes, present or future for scheduled ones. Active voice where a human actor matters.
- No "we're excited to announce." State the change.
- No importance puffery. The reader will decide if it matters to them.

## Documentation and README

- Imperative for instructions. "Run the migration" beats "You should run the migration."
- Passive is often correct here and is not a defect: "The config is read at startup" when no actor matters.
- No narrative openers. No "In today's fast-moving development landscape."
- Code, commands, and identifiers are untouchable under the scope guards.
- Consistency beats variety. Synonym cycling is actively harmful in docs.

## Cover letters, bios, and personal statements

- Specifics only. A generic bio sentence that adds no specific fact fails the portability test and wastes the line.
- No "passionate about." Name what you have built or worked on.
- Keep first-person voice and admitted uncertainty. Polished-to-generic is the standard failure here.
- Do not inflate the writer's record. Factual accuracy and meaning apply with full force.
