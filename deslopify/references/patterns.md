# Patterns

Every entry is a pattern, why it reads as machine-written, and the fix. Apply the precedence order from SKILL.md first. A pattern that survives factual accuracy, meaning, explicit user instruction, or writer voice stays in the draft.

## Openers and setups

**Throat-clearing openers.** "Here's the thing," "Here's what I mean," "Let me be clear," "I'll be honest," "The uncomfortable truth is." These announce that a point is coming instead of making it. Cut and state the point.

**Faux-insight setups.** "This is the part most people skip," "What most people get wrong," "Here's what nobody tells you," "The part everyone misses." These flatter the writer as the lone expert who sees clearly. Cut the setup and let the claim stand.
> "The part everyone misses: distribution is the real moat" → "Distribution is the moat."

**Rhetorical setups.** "What if I told you...", "Think about it:", "Plot twist:", and self-answered "Question? Answer." pairs. Drop them and make the point.

**Meta-joiners.** "The rest of this piece will...", "Before we get into that...", "With that out of the way..." Delete. Let the piece move.

## Fake structure

**Binary contrasts.** "This is not X. It's Y." / "The question isn't X, it's Y." / "It's not just X but Y." The contrast manufactures tension around a point that could be stated flat.
> "The question isn't the model. It's the eval." → "The eval matters more than the model."

**Negative listing.** "Not a X. Not a Y. A Z." Say Z.

**Colon reveals.** A noun phrase, a colon, then a lowercase dramatic reveal.
> "The detail that makes it work: a separate agent grades it." → "A separate agent does the grading, which is what makes it work."

Colons are for lists, labels, and quotes. Prefer sentence case after a colon unless grammar, a proper noun, a title, or code requires otherwise.

**Dramatic fragmentation.** "X. And Y. And Z." or "That's it. That's the whole thing." Use complete sentences.

**Synonym cycling.** Rotating terms for variety makes the reader check whether three things are being described.
> "The agent reviews the draft. The assistant scores the piece. The tool suggests fixes." → "The agent reviews the draft, scores it, and suggests fixes."

## Fake substance

**Superficial analysis.** Trailing `-ing` clauses that pretend to explain significance: "highlighting," "underscoring," "reflecting," "showcasing," "demonstrating."
> "The launch adds file search, highlighting the team's commitment to better workflows" → "The launch adds file search, so users can find old drafts without leaving the editor."

**Importance puffery.** "Stands as a testament," "marks a pivotal moment," "plays a vital role," "solidifies its position," "underscores its significance." State the fact and let the reader judge.
> "The launch marks a pivotal moment for the company" → "The launch is the company's first paid product."

**Interpretive metadiscourse.** Lines that step outside the subject to tell the reader what to notice or how much weight to give it: "That last part matters more than it sounds," "The key point is," "As you can see," "This distinction matters," redundant "In other words." If the point is clear, delete. If it is not, add support instead of instruction.

**Vague declaratives.** "The reasons are structural." "The implications are significant." "There are tradeoffs here." Name the reason, the implication, the tradeoff.

**Fake-strong verbs.** Inflated verbs where "is" or "has" is clearer.
> "The app serves as a centralized hub for sponsor management" → "The app tracks sponsors, drafts, due dates, and approvals in one place."

**Lazy extremes.** "Every," "always," "never," "no one," "everyone" doing vague work. Either the absolute is true and load-bearing, or it should be a number.

**Portability failure.** A **claim, framing, or self-description** that could move unchanged to another person, company, country, or product. Cut it or replace it with a fact, mechanism, consequence, or judgment specific to this subject.

Applies to marketing copy, landing pages, bios, cover letters, and the framing sentences of posts and essays. Does **not** apply to accurate factual, procedural, or behavioral statements. "The config is read at startup" is portable because the behavior is ordinary, not because the sentence is empty. Reusable wording is filler only when it asserts nothing checkable about this subject.

The test is whether the sentence states something, not whether a competitor could truthfully say it too.

**False agency.** An abstraction is given intention, judgment, or feeling that only a person can have, which lets the sentence avoid naming who acted. "The decision emerged." "The complaint becomes a fix." "The roadmap wants to prioritize onboarding." "Concerns were felt across the org." Find the person and name them.

The test is misattributed intention or emotion, not verb class. A system performing its specified behavior is not false agency, however human the verb looks: see "Institutional and system actors" below. If you cannot name a plausible human actor, the sentence is probably describing a system correctly and should be left alone.

**Narrator from a distance.** "People often find," "Many teams struggle with," "Nobody designed this." Put the reader in the scene. "You" beats "People" in most formats. Check `formats.md` first, since some formats want the third person.

## Fake attribution

**Weasel attribution.** "Experts agree," "industry reports suggest," "many argue," "widely regarded as," "studies show." Name the source or cut the attribution. If the source is absent from the draft and the user did not ask for research, remove the attributed population claim rather than leaving it as a warning; preserve the writer's separately supported claim, including a first-person admission joined to the attribution. Reconnect its subject explicitly when removing the population clause would leave a dangling pronoun; retain expressive words that carry self-reproach. If the remaining copy depends on that claim, use BLOCKED and name the missing source. Never invent one or soften an invented one into "some have suggested."

## Endings

**Fake-profound kickers.** The final "deep" line that turns the point into a metaphor, aphorism, or mic-drop. Delete it. Do not rewrite it into a better metaphor. Do not preserve its rhythm. End on the clearest concrete sentence already in the draft. A causal-sounding maxim is still redundant when the preceding example already demonstrates its entire lesson: ask what new fact, action, or personal stance the final line adds. If none, remove it. Do not replace it with an invented action just to supply closure. Preserve an explicit user-requested takeaway or a distinct personal judgment.

**Summary-recap endings.** "In conclusion," "Ultimately," "Overall," or a final paragraph restating the piece. The reader was just there.

---

## Rhythm and formatting

**Robotic rhythm.** Repeated sentence shapes, identical paragraph structures, stacked punchy fragments, every paragraph the same length. Vary the shape where it helps the point, not everywhere.

**Formatting slop.** Emoji in headings, bold sprinkled mid-sentence, bullet lists where two sentences of prose read better, headers over two-sentence sections, tables with one meaningful column. Format follows content.

**Em dashes.** Not a default rhythm crutch. None in short copy. One or two in a long draft when a dash clearly beats a comma, period, or parenthesis. Remove clusters.

## What is not a pattern

These get flagged by cruder rules and should not be:

- **Adverbs as a category.** "Not," "only," "still," "already," "instead," "however," "tomorrow" are adverbs. Only the list in `words.md` is suspect, and only when it adds nothing.
- **Passive voice with an unknown or irrelevant actor.** "The server was compromised at 3am" is correct if that is what is known.
- **Institutional and system actors.** "The scheduler decides which job runs next," "the parser rejects malformed input," "the court ruled," "the compiler infers the type," "the service enforces the quota." Software, protocols, courts, organizations, and instruments act through their specified behavior. These are more accurate than inventing a human, and rewriting them around a developer misdescribes runtime behavior.
- **Non-human grammatical subjects.** "The build takes 40 minutes" is fine.
- **Questions, or sentences opening with when/why/what.** "When the build fails, check the logs" is fine. The real targets are the faux-insight and colon-reveal patterns above.
- **Three items in a list.** If there are three things, name three.
- **A memorable sentence.** A line can land well and be true. Fake-profound kickers are defined by function, which is substituting a metaphor for the point at the end, not by being quotable.
